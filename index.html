Figures → xy data

Turning published plots back into the numbers they were drawn from, and checking every one of them. Two dashboards — the whole-PDF pipeline and the figure-level engine it is built on — and two comparisons, where the approaches that lost are shown beside the one that won, with the numbers that decided it.

Papers → xy data

Start from a PDF. Every figure region is rendered, split into panels by axis geometry, classified by a vision model, digitised by the pixel engine, and each curve's identity recovered from the paper's own text — so a curve labelled 1a comes out named. The classifier also says how each panel is drawn — lines, symbols, or both — and that picks the tracer. The whole corpus, line plots digitised; the 53 panels drawn as symbols are classified and listed as a worklist rather than read. Includes what was tried at each stage, and where it still falls short.

Scatter readers compared

The hard case, three ways. Our reader groups whole symbols by colour, size and fill; two others are adapted from WebPlotDigitizer — one keeps every pixel within a radius of a reference colour, the other recognises a symbol by matching its shape against the binary mask and only consults colour afterwards. Same panels, same axes, side by side, so you can judge by eye which reads a faint scatter best.

Scatter: pixels vs model, whole corpus

Every scatter panel read twice, and judged on measures chosen not to presuppose that denser is better — because the first ones did. On the typical panel the model's sparse points sit half a percent of the plot box from real data and reproduce the curve to a few percent, which is enough for a trend. The tail is the problem: a quarter of panels fall outside 5%, and a few are wrong by orders of magnitude with nothing in the model's own output to flag which.
